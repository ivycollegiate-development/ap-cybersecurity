## PART 2 — THE README, WHICH IS ACTUALLY THE PORTFOLIO (14 min)

**The README is the portfolio.** Not one file inside it. It is the first thing a reviewer opens, it is what they judge in thirty seconds, and in a repo it is what appears on the landing page.

**What a good README does, in this order, and this is the structure:**

**1. One line on who I am and what this is.** Not a biography. *"AP Cybersecurity student, 2026–27. This is what I did and what I found."* Then get out of the way.

**2. The through-line sentence.** *"I am a defensive-leaning generalist who is drawn to detection and log analysis."* **Put it near the top, in plain language.** This is what makes a folder into a portfolio, and a reviewer who reads only that line should have a correct impression.

**3. What is here, as a table.** Every piece: title, what it is, what skill it demonstrates, and a link. **The "what skill" column is not decoration** — it is what lets a reviewer see the shape of you at a glance, and it is the thing most portfolios omit.

**4. Three featured pieces, with one paragraph each.** The paragraph follows the same shape as the capstone piece: situation, question, reasoning, what I would do differently. **A reader should get the substance without opening the file**, and if they do open it, it goes deeper.

**5. The methodology notes.** This is what makes the portfolio credible rather than a gallery: the two log-analysis method cards and the web assessment card, as short pages. **Sharing your method is a mark of professional confidence** — it says here is how I work, and you can check whether it holds up.

**6. The honest limitations.** What this portfolio is not: not a penetration test report, not a production codebase, not a claim of professional experience. **One short paragraph of this makes everything else more credible, not less.** A portfolio that claims to be more than it is invites a reader to look for the gap; a portfolio that names its own limits is trusted on everything else.

**7. Contact and links.**

**Design rules that are not optional:**
- ☐ **A stranger must be able to navigate it in thirty seconds.** Headers, a table, links that work. This is a reading test, not a design test.
- ☐ **Every screenshot gets a caption explaining what it shows and why you are including it.** An uncaptioned screenshot is noise; a captioned one is evidence. **Do not include screenshots you cannot explain** — that rule removes about half of them and improves the portfolio.
- ☐ **Check every link works.** Every one. An open invitation link in a portfolio read by a technical reviewer is a self-inflicted wound.
- ☐ **Write the README as though a stranger with no context will read it, because that is exactly who will.** No internal references, no "as discussed in class," no assumed knowledge. **This applies even to people who will know who you are** — a portfolio gets forwarded without commentary.

## PART 3 — POLISH TIME (18 min)

**Order of work, and the order matters because the earlier items unblock the later ones:**

1. **Finish the capstone piece (20% of the time).** It should be near-final from May 14. If it is not, this is where it happens, and it happens first because the README cannot describe a piece that does not exist yet.
2. **Write the second featured piece (30%).** Session 7's log analysis writeup is the natural choice — the template is on your card from May 13, the method is sound, and it demonstrates a different skill from the capstone. **If you cannot finish it, publish it as a partial with an honest note saying so.** A labelled partial is credible; a silent incomplete piece is not.
3. **Third piece (10%).** Whichever of your CTF writeups is strongest. **If none of them is written up yet, pick the session you remember best and write it up in 20 minutes** — one piece at a high standard beats three at a low one.
4. **The archive section (15%).** Every remaining artifact, one line each, with an honest description. **This is where the volume of the year becomes visible**, and it is genuinely impressive: eight CTF sessions, five labs, a capstone, a year of study. The archive makes the trajectory argument that no single piece can.
5. **README, and commit (25%).** As above. Commit as you go — the history is evidence.

**Three polish passes, in this order, and they are different passes:**
- **Pass 1 — accuracy.** Every technical claim is either something you did, something you observed, or something you checked. **If you cannot verify it, cut it or mark it as an expectation.** This is the pass that protects your credibility, and it is the pass students skip because it requires checking rather than writing.
- **Pass 2 — completeness.** A stranger reading this should never wonder what you mean. Expand every acronym on first use. State the assumptions. Fill the gaps where you relied on context nobody else has.
- **Pass 3 — concision.** Cut 20%. Most of what I will cut in a portfolio is the throat-clearing, the restating, and the hedging. **A shorter document that says the same thing is a better document**, and cutting is a skill.

**If you finish early:** write the questions you expect on May 28 and prepare the answers. Or improve a piece you already know is your weakest.

## PART 4 — THE MAY 28 CHECK (8 min)

**Run the portfolio the way a reviewer will, and time it.**

- ☐ **Thirty-second read.** Show the README to someone who has not seen it. After thirty seconds, they must be able to answer: *what does this person do, and what is the best thing here?* If they cannot, the README is not doing its job — fix the top before anything else.
- ☐ **One-click check.** Can they reach your best piece from the README in one click? One. Not two.
- ☐ **Every link opens.** Every one. Test them.
- ☐ **Your three-minute talk, out loud, standing, timed.** With the actual portfolio open in front of you. It goes in the middle of the presentation and it is the part that carries the five minutes.
- ☐ **The opening and the close, memorised.** Not the middle — the middle can be conversational. **The first thirty seconds and the last thirty seconds should be reliable**, because those are the parts you will be worst at if you are nervous.
- ☐ **Your three anticipated questions, with answers.** Written down. You drafted these on May 17; refine them tonight.

**Then hand it to me. Tomorrow it goes to a peer reviewer, and the review is only as good as what they can find.**

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief, on how to present local context to an international reader.**

You are presenting on May 28 to a room that includes people who have never been to Taiwan and people who have. **Write for the first group, respect the second.**

**The technique, and it is a real one: define the term, then use it.** *"A semiconductor foundry — a facility that manufactures chips for other companies, as opposed to an integrated device manufacturer that designs them as well — makes the concentration of advanced capability in this region the kind of geopolitical fact that shapes the entire local threat model."* **One clause of context, then you are speaking your reader's language for the rest of the talk.** That is professional communication and it is a learnable skill.

**The three failure modes, all of them common among candidates from outside the US:**
- **The untranslated acronym.** A reader who does not know the local industry structure cannot follow your threat model, and they will nod rather than admit it.
- **The pride inflection.** Presenting local context as a *strength* reads as defensiveness to some audiences and as genuine advantage to others. **State it neutrally and let the evidence carry it** — the strongest version of this is a portfolio where the local work is simply the best work, not the work that is explained most loudly.
- **The unearned superlative.** Any claim that cannot be sourced — and that includes "attacked more than any other country" — is a liability in a technical room. **The specific, checkable version of your local advantage is worth far more than the impressive version that cannot be defended.**

**The one line worth getting right in your presentation, if you want one:** *"I work on the local threat landscape, and the local analysis is not the same analysis."* Then show one piece of evidence for it. That is a memorable, defensible, specific claim, and it is true of your portfolio.
