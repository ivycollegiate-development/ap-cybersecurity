## PART 1 — THE RULES OF A USEFUL DEBRIEF (4 min)

Three rules, on the board, and they govern the whole period:

1. **The exam is not your worth.** Say it once, out loud, together, and then let it go. We are not here to rank the room. A debrief that turns into a score comparison is worthless and slightly harmful.
2. **Honest beats comfortable.** A vague self-assessment produces a vague plan, and a vague plan produces nothing.
3. **The exam is a sample, not a verdict.** One administration, on a particular day, under particular conditions, on a specific form. It is data about one sitting. It is also, for most of you, the single most important line on an application — but those two facts sit together without contradiction.

**Then answer, in writing, one question, at length:**

> **What did the exam actually ask — as opposed to what we drilled?**

Be concrete. Not "it was hard." Specific examples of the *shape* of the surprise:

- ☐ Did it ask for a specific control where you had prepared a category? *(The most common miss, and the one we spent two days on.)*
- ☐ Did it give you an artifact in a format you had not practised reading?
- ☐ Did the scenario include a detail you did not know was load-bearing?
- ☐ Did the free response have more sub-parts than you expected, or fewer?
- ☐ Was the time pressure different from what you planned for?
- ☐ Did anything on it surprise you that you genuinely could not have prepared for?

**That last checkbox is important and I want it answered honestly.** There is content on any exam that no review block can reach, and there is a point in admitting which items those were. Beating yourself up for them is useless; knowing where your preparation had a ceiling is genuinely useful information.

**Then, the three things.** This is the graded artifact:

> **Three things you would drill differently.**

Rules for these three, so they are worth something:
- Each must be a **change to a behaviour or a method**, not a wish. "Study more" is not one. "I would write the layer before the control every time" is.
- Each must be **specific enough to act on** within one week.
- Each must be **yours** — if all three are the same, you have written one thing three times and I will ask.

**Then the reverse question, and this is the one people skip:** **three things we drilled that the exam rewarded.** Naming what worked is not ego, it is the mechanism by which you decide what to keep doing. A student who leaves knowing what worked and what did not is far better equipped than one who leaves only knowing what hurt.

## PART 3 — THE FIVE-MINUTE PIECE (16 min)

**Work in pairs. This is the actual skill.** In an interview, a candidate who says "the exam was hard" is uninteresting; a candidate who can say "here is what the exam asked, here is what I got wrong, here is what I changed, here is the evidence I have since produced" is hireable. That is the whole portfolio thesis in one sentence, and it starts today.

**Partner A, four minutes, no notes, answering to B:** the three changes, in order, and for each, one sentence on *why it was that change rather than another*.

**Swap. Partner B, four minutes.**

**Partner B then asks, for each of the three:** *"what would evidence look like that you actually did it?"* And Partner A answers. **This is the pressure-test, and it is the part that converts a reflection into a plan.** A change with no observable evidence is a wish. Ask the question; make your partner answer it.

**Four minutes left:** each person writes down the one change that survived the pressure test, and the evidence they will produce. That becomes the opening of their portfolio.

**Why a pair for this, and not solo:** solo reflection is generous to yourself. A partner asking "what would prove it?" is the cheapest quality control in education, and it is the same pressure a hiring manager will apply.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief.** One current TWNCERT or iThome item from the last two weeks.

**The question, and it is the debrief question:** *what would this incident's response have looked like, written by someone who took this exam?*

Push it further, because the answer is the point: **name the detection source, name the control, name the notification obligation, and name the timeline.** You have all four. You can now write a short incident note in the structure a real organisation would use, and that is a sentence you can put in a portfolio.

**And the local angle worth a portfolio of its own:** Taiwan is one of the most heavily targeted places in the world for phishing, ransomware, and semiconductor-focused intrusion — and the publicly written analyses of those incidents, many in Chinese, are a body of threat knowledge that a candidate who reads only English-language vendor reports simply does not have. If you read one of those analyses this week, that is portfolio material. Say so in one sentence.
