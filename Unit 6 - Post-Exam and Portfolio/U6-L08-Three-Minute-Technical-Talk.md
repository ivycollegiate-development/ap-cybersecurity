## PART 1 — WHY THE WRITTEN PORTFOLIO IS NOT ENOUGH (8 min)

Say the uncomfortable thing first, because it is true and it will save you on May 28.

**A portfolio gets you the interview. The interview gets you the job. And in the interview, you are not reading.** You have thirty seconds of a stranger's attention and a question like *"tell me about a piece of work you've done."*

**The gap:** a candidate with an excellent written portfolio and a vague spoken answer loses. The writing demonstrates thinking. The speaking demonstrates **whether you actually own the thinking** — and there is a visible difference between a person describing their work and a person who did it. Interviewers are, in a blunt sense, checking for the second.

**And the technical case specifically:** a portfolio piece is graded on a document that cannot be questioned. A talk is graded on your ability to hold up under a question. *Why did you choose that control? What would you do differently? How do you know that finding is real?* If you cannot answer those about your own capstone, you did not write it — you assembled it, and that will come out in the room.

**So today is about two skills: structure, and surviving a question.**

## PART 2 — THE THREE-MINUTE STRUCTURE (14 min)

**Three minutes. That is the unit to build against.** Not five — the portfolio presentation on May 28 gives you five minutes for the whole portfolio, so the technical talk is a component, and three minutes is the right size for one piece of work.

**The structure. Five parts, with the time each should take:**

**0:00–0:20 — The hook (20 sec).** One sentence, concrete, that makes the listener care. Not "I want to talk about the capstone" — that is a title, not a hook.
- ✗ *"My capstone was about a semiconductor fab."*
- ✓ *"We had to defend a chip fab, and the hardest part wasn't technical — it was that the real constraint was how many hours of downtime the plant could absorb."*

**0:20–0:50 — The situation and the question (30 sec).** Two sentences. The scenario, and what was actually being asked. **This is the part people skip and it is the part that makes the next two minutes comprehensible.** A listener who does not know the scope will not follow your reasoning and will not be able to ask a good question.

**0:50–2:00 — The reasoning (70 sec).** The longest segment, and it is the whole point. **What you decided, and why.** Not the diagram — the decision. One decision explained well beats three explained quickly. Structure it as: *here was the choice; here were the options; here is why this one, because of this constraint.*

**2:00–2:30 — The honest part (30 sec).** What you got wrong, or what you would do differently. **Say it plainly, do not apologise for it, and do not perform false modesty.** A speaker who says "the main limitation was that I under-modelled the physical access threat" sounds like a practitioner. A speaker who says "I could have done better in some areas" sounds like someone reading a form.

**2:30–3:00 — The close (30 sec).** What you learned that generalises, and one line on where you are going. This is your through-line sentence, spoken.

**The discipline, and it is the hard one:** **three minutes is very little time and you will want to fill it with more detail.** Resist. A three-minute talk with one well-explained decision beats a three-minute talk with four rushed ones, because the rushed ones produce no questions — and questions are where interviews are actually won.

## PART 3 — BUILD AND REHEARSE (16 min)

**Draft: five minutes. Rehear: eight minutes. Three pairs, three rotations.**

**Draft (5 min).** One page, the five parts with a word budget for each. Write the sentences, not bullets — **you cannot speak from bullets, and finding the sentence on the day is where talks fall apart.** Then write down, at the bottom, the three questions you most expect to be asked about this work. **Write them now, while you are still close to the argument.** For a capstone talk they will be something like: why that architecture, what would you have done with more time, how do you know the threat model is reasonable.

**Rehearse (8 min), in threes.** Rotate through: A talks (3 min), B asks one question (30 sec), C gives feedback (60 sec), then rotate. Three rotations total.

**The rules for the listener, and these make the exercise work:**
- ☐ **Interrupt at 3:00.** Hard stop, even mid-sentence. **The most common real-world failure is running over, and you cannot practise the stop if nobody enforces it.**
- ☐ Ask your question and **do not rescue the speaker.** Silence after a question is uncomfortable and productive. Do not rephrase it, do not hint, do not let them off the hook.
- ☐ Feedback in one minute, and it must be **two specific things.** Not "that was good" — *"you said 'the architecture had several layers' at 1:10 and a listener could not picture any of them."*

**The rules for the speaker:**
- ☐ **Time your own talk. Out loud, standing.** Talking silently in your head runs about 20% faster than talking out loud, and the difference is the difference between finishing and being cut off.
- ☐ If you are asked something you cannot fully answer, **say so and say what you would need to find out.** *"I don't know — I'd want to look at whether that control is in the default configuration or the hardened one, and that's a good question."* That answer is **better** in an interview than a confident guess, and it costs you nothing to learn now.

## PART 4 — THE MAY 28 PRESENTATION, PLANNED (3 min)

You present the whole portfolio for five minutes on May 28. Sketch it now, in four lines, so you are not designing it on the day:

- ☐ **0:00–0:30** — Who I am and what I did this year. The one-page front, spoken.
- ☐ **0:30–3:30** — **The 3-minute talk you build today.** This is the centre of the presentation. It is your strongest piece and it gets the most time.
- ☐ **3:30–4:30** — Two other pieces, briefly: what they are and what they show. One line each. **Do not summarise them — point at them and let the portfolio do the work.**
- ☐ **4:30–5:00** — The through-line, and what you are doing next. Then stop. **Do not trail off; a clean stop is a professional signal.**

**Five minutes exactly, and finishing early is better than finishing over.** Q&A follows, and the five minutes are what the other listeners judged you on while you were presenting.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief, and this one is about *how* to talk, not what about.**

**The local advantage, stated as a speaking skill rather than a knowledge claim:** a candidate who can explain a Taiwanese semiconductor or data-protection context to an international listener, in English, without either exaggerating it or apologising for it, is doing something genuinely difficult. That is a communication skill, and it is rarer than the technical knowledge.

**Practise it in this lesson:**
- ☐ Pick one Taiwan-specific element of your capstone and **explain it in one sentence to a partner who has never been there.** No jargon, no acronyms, no assumed context. If your partner had to ask a follow-up to understand it, the sentence failed.
- ☐ Then say it again with the jargon restored. **The gap between those two versions is the skill**, and being able to move between them is what makes someone credible in an international room.

**The failure modes to avoid, all of them common:**
- **Overclaiming.** "Taiwan is attacked more than any other country" is not a claim you can source, and inflating a real advantage destroys it. Say what you can support.
- **Apologising for it.** Do not say "I know my experience is only a school project, but…" — you are a student and everyone in the room knows it. State the work plainly.
- **Flattening it into a generality.** "In my country, cyberattacks are a concern" is a sentence that could have come from anywhere and therefore says nothing.

**The one that works, and you can build it today:** name the specific thing, say why it matters, and stop. *"The concentration of advanced fabrication in this region makes it a target where the adversary's goal is disruption rather than theft, and that changes which controls are worth their cost."* Twenty-five seconds, defensible, and unmistakably local.
