## PART 1 — THE HONEST PICTURE BEFORE THE PITCH (8 min)

Three things said plainly, before the descriptions, so the descriptions are read correctly:

**One: no lane is an easy first job.** Every one of these starts somewhere unglamorous. The SOC lane starts watching an alert queue. The pentest lane starts writing scripts that do the boring parts. The GRC lane starts reading policy documents and building spreadsheets. The engineering lane starts being the person nobody pages for months.

**Two: the first two years are about breadth, not depth.** You are not going to be a good anything yet. What you are going to do is learn how a real environment actually works, which is not in any textbook and is the single most valuable thing you can acquire.

**Three: the jobs are changing, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.** Much routine output work is being automated. What is being *demanded* is judgement, accountability, and the ability to reason about a system nobody has written a playbook for. This course's through-line — being the person accountable for a risk decision — is the durable skill across every lane below.

## PART 2 — THE FIVE LANES (18 min)

For each: what the work actually is, what a first year looks like, the honest downside, and what it takes to get there. **Read the real job descriptions where marked — not my description, the posting.**

### 1. SOC Analyst (Security Operations Centre)

**The work:** monitoring alerts, triaging, deciding what is a real incident, escalating, and writing it up. Detection engineering sits above this; this is tier one.
**A first year:** alert queue rotation, a lot of tuning, learning what normal looks like for your environment, and slowly being trusted with a real decision at 3 AM.
**The honest downside:** it can be a volume job. If your organisation is under-resourced, "analyst" can mean "the person who clears the queue," and the judgement work goes to a team three time zones away.
**Get there:** a security-minded IT support or sysadmin role at an organisation with a real SOC, plus your writeups. This is the ladder from the Credential Ladder.
**Evidence to look for:** search SOC analyst postings and read the requirements. What fraction ask for Security+? What do they ask you to have *done*? **Record the actual counts — this is real data, and real data is the argument.**

### 2. Penetration Tester

**The work:** attacking systems with authorisation, in scope, documenting what you find and what it means, and helping the team fix it.
**A first year:** you are the person running the scanner and writing the tickets, and the pentest is the part you will grow into.
**The honest downside:** it is the most crowded lane relative to the number of entry-level openings, it is portfolio-dependent to an unusual degree, and the people who look like they skipped a rung usually did not.
**Get there:** PenTest+ or equivalent as a floor, then a practical, portfolio-heavy certification, plus a real body of documented work. **This lane is the one where the portfolio is not an accessory — it is the credential.**
**Evidence to look for:** read pentest job postings and count how many ask for demonstrated lab or CTF experience versus certifications.

### 3. Incident Response

**The work:** the work that happens when something has already happened. Containment, eradication, evidence preservation, communication, and the writeup that goes to people who will ask what happened and why.
**A first year:** shadowing, helping with evidence handling, learning the playbook you will one day run.
**The honest downside:** IR is irregular, sometimes intense, and in smaller organisations you may be the only person, which is both an opportunity and a genuinely bad first experience if you are not ready for it.
**Get there:** SOC experience first — **IR is usually a specialisation *of* detection work, not a separate entry point.** Certs like GCIH/GCFA come after you have incidents under your belt, not before.
**Evidence to look for:** IR postings and how they describe entry — most will tell you honestly that they want detection experience first.

### 4. GRC — Governance, Risk, and Compliance

**The work:** deciding what the organisation's risk actually is, translating regulation into requirements, running audits, producing attestations, and being the person who signs or advises on whether a control is adequate.
**A first year:** policy and control mapping, evidence collection, spreadsheets, and a great deal of reading. It looks like the least technical lane and it is not.
**The honest downside:** it can be dull if you let it be, and it attracts people who mistake paperwork for security. **Done well it is the most durable lane on this list** — regulation does not automate away, and someone must be accountable.
**Get there:** Security+ as a floor, then CISA or CISM. **Unusually AI-resistant, and the writing skill is the differentiator** — a person who can write a defensible risk memo is genuinely scarce.
**Evidence to look for:** read GRC postings and notice how many are written for people who can communicate rather than people who can exploit.

### 5. Security Engineering

**The work:** building the things — secure defaults, detection tooling, infrastructure as code with security in, tooling that makes the secure path the easy path for other engineers.
**A first year:** you are a competent generalist developer or sysadmin who is good at security, which is a rarer and more valuable thing than it sounds.
**The honest downside:** the highest bar of the five, and the one most likely to require a degree or an equivalent body of evidence. The catch: it is also the lane with the most leverage and the most transferable to adjacent fields.
**Get your:** software fundamentals and systems fundamentals, security depth, and a portfolio that shows you have **built** something rather than only analysed it.
**Evidence to look for:** what the postings actually list as requirements, and how many of those requirements you could meet today.

## PART 3 — WRITE THE CAREER MEMO (14 min)

**This is the deliverable, and it is a memo, not a fantasy.** Format: four sections, one page, written in prose with at most one bulleted list. Prose on purpose — you are practising the skill that lane 4 is built on and that all five lanes reward.

**1. The lane.** One of the five. Name it, and give the reason as evidence from your own work, not from this lesson. *"SOC, because the log analysis I did in Saturday Session 7 is the closest thing to real work I have done, and I want more of it."* That is an answer. *"SOC because it sounds cool"* is not.

**2. The realistic path from here.** Not the fantasy version. Write it as: the first job, the thing that gets me into it, the thing that gets me out of it, and roughly how long. Be honest that the first job is probably not the one on the poster. **Name the specific organisation type** you would aim at — one with a security team, at a size where you would actually be visible — because "a company with a security team" is a strategy and "a mid-size company with a 24/7 SOC" is a plan.

**3. The gap.** What you do not have yet, honestly, and the single next thing that closes it. One thing. You have three build days and thirteen days left; the gap you name should be one you can actually work on.

**4. The through-line.** Your portfolio sentence from Monday, updated. Where does this lane sit inside it? **If the lane contradicts the through-line, one of the two needs to change, and that is a useful thing to discover this week rather than in September.**

**Method:** write the first draft in ten minutes without stopping, then revise for five. The first draft finds the argument; the revision makes it readable. Do not write it in order.

**Then, in pairs, five minutes each:** read your partner's memo aloud. The partner's only job is to answer one question — *"what would I have to believe to hire this person based on this memo?"* — and to say whether they could.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief, on careers specifically.**

**Question:** *which of these five lanes has a distinctly different shape in Taiwan, and why?*

Points worth your own research and thought, and worth putting in a portfolio:

- **Defensive and IR demand is shaped by the local threat landscape.** Semiconductor concentration and heavy targeting mean the organisations doing serious defensive work here face a threat model that is not generic — and experience in it is not transferable *from* elsewhere, only *to* elsewhere.
- **The foundry and supply-chain ecosystem** creates defensive, industrial, and supply-chain-security roles that do not map onto a standard US job ladder. If you are interested in semiconductors specifically, this is where to look, and it is a place a US-only candidate has no advantage.
- **Regulation and data-protection practice** creates real GRC demand with a local statutory base, and a candidate who understands the local legal frame has something a generic candidate does not.

**The portfolio point, and it is the one to use:** your through-line can legitimately be *"I work on local threat landscape."* That is a defensible specialisation, it is one you can actually evidence with the writeups from this year, and it is the kind of thing that makes you memorable in a room full of candidates who all read the same material.

**The honest counterpoint again, because it keeps mattering:** a local specialisation is a *differentiator*, not a *substitute*. The fundamentals still get you the interview. Do not let a compelling narrative about local advantage become a reason to skip the unglamorous first job.
