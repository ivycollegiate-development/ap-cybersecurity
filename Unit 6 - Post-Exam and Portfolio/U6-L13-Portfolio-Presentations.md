## PART 1 — BEFORE WE START (5 min)

**Housekeeping, quickly, because it is genuinely important:**

- ☐ Your portfolio repo must be **public and reachable right now.** Check it on your phone if you have to. **An unreachable portfolio is the one failure that turns a strong year into an invisible one.**
- ☐ Your three-minute talk's **opening thirty seconds and closing thirty seconds are memorised.** The middle can be conversational.
- ☐ Presentation order and time slots are on the board. **Respect other people's time** — five minutes means stop at five minutes, and I will stop you, kindly but absolutely.
- ☐ Everyone is a listener for the other presentations, and that is a job. **Listen the way you would want to be listened to.** Specific attention is the most generous thing you can give someone on their last class presentation of a year.

**The one-minute frame, and I want to say it before anybody starts rather than in a post-mortem:**

Today is not an audition. **You are presenting work you did, and the room already knows it, because you have been in it for a year.** Nobody here is deciding whether you are competent — that decision was made across a hundred and thirty periods. What happens today is that you learn what it feels like to stand up and say *"here is what I did, here is how I reasoned about it, and here is what I got wrong."* **That skill is worth more than anything else on the CED list, and you will use it in every interview, every introduction, and every disagreement for the rest of your working life.** So do it properly, and take the questions seriously even when they are hard.

## PART 2 — THE FORMAT (40 min)

**Five minutes, per the frame you rehearsed:**

- **0:00–0:30** — Who I am and what I did this year. Strongest piece, named. The README spoken.
- **0:30–3:30** — **The three-minute talk.** The centre. Your best piece: the situation, the question, the reasoning, the honest part, the close.
- **3:30–4:30** — Two other pieces, one line each. **Point, do not summarise.**
- **4:30–5:00** — The through-line and what is next. **Stop.**

**Then Q&A, 2–3 minutes.** Questions from the room and from me. **The questions I will ask, so you can prepare honestly rather than guess:**
- ☐ *Which piece here best represents how you actually think, and why that one?*
- ☐ *What did you get wrong, and how did you find out?*
- ☐ *What would you do differently if you started this today?*
- ☐ *Tell me about a thing you built, not a thing you analysed.*
- ☐ *What do you want to be doing in two years, and what in this portfolio says you're on the way there?*

**The last one deserves real preparation, and the honest answer is the good one.** "I want to be doing X, and this portfolio shows I am on the way because of Y, and it also shows the gap Z I still have to close." Naming the gap is not weakness; it is the single strongest credibility move available to you.

**How to take a question, and this is worth saying out loud:**
- **Listen to the whole question before answering.** The answer is often in the second half.
- **If you do not know, say so and say how you would find out.** *"I don't know — I'd want to check whether that's a default or a hardened setting, and that's a fair question."* **In a technical room this is a strong answer. A confident guess is the only thing that reliably loses you.**
- **If you disagree with the premise, say so politely and explain.** Disagreement is a professional act when it is reasoned.
- **Never defend the work as if it were you.** *"That's not quite right — what I actually did was…"* and correct the record. That correction is a credibility gain, not a loss of face.

## PART 3 — THE CLOSE: WHAT YOU LEAVE WITH (5 min)

**Say this to the room, and it is meant literally, because these are the last words of a year:**

**You now have something nine months ago you did not have.** A public body of work with history behind it, a written record of how you think, a method you can hand to someone else, and a demonstrated ability to take a piece of technical work and make a stranger understand it. **That last one is the rare one, and it is the one that determines how much of your work ever gets read.**

**And here is the honest part, which belongs at the end of a year like this one.** The strongest thing in this room was never the exam. The strongest thing was Saturday May 1, when you cracked a hash you did not know how to crack, and wrote up the attempt that failed as carefully as the one that worked. **That is the professional habit, and it is worth more than the score.**

**Three things to carry, and they are not course content:**
1. **Name the limit of what you know.** Say it before someone else finds it. It is always better coming from you.
2. **Show the reasoning, not just the conclusion.** The conclusion is cheap; the reasoning is the work.
3. **Write it down.** The person who documents the reasoning is the person who gets trusted with more of it.

**And the practical coda, because you are leaving this building:**
- ☐ **The AP exam happened May 5.** Your score and your credit, when they arrive, depend on nothing else. This course has no final.
- ☐ **Portfolio stays live.** Update it. The next thing you do — a CTF, a lab, a certification, a class project — gets added. **A portfolio you stop updating is a portfolio that dies, and the whole point of a year of this is that it keeps compounding.**
- ☐ **Saturday sessions continue where they are.** The series does not end with the school year; if you want the mentorship, ask.
- ☐ **Keep the credential ladder in view.** The voucher path, the writeups, the ladder from support into security. You wrote it; now use it.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief, the last one of the year, and it is a closing one.**

**One question, asked to the room, and answered by a few of you aloud:** *what is the thing you learned this year that you could not have learned from a textbook, and that a candidate from a less-targeted country could not learn either?*

**The answers I am looking for, and they are all in this room somewhere in the last thirteen days:**
- A local incident you read and understood, rather than a summary of one.
- A threat model where the local context genuinely changed which controls were worth their cost.
- A compliance question where the framework was different, and working out whose law applied.
- A Saturday session where the challenge was hard and you wrote up the failure honestly.
- A capstone where you chose a sector and the local reality of that sector made your architecture better.

**And the thing worth saying at the end, to a room of students in Taiwan, in May:**

**Taiwan is one of the most heavily targeted places in the world for cyberattack, and that is usually framed as a problem.** It is also the reason a graduate of this room has something to say that most graduates of most cyber programmes cannot. You know what a foundry floor is. You know what a local notification obligation is. You know what a phishing lure aimed at this population looks like, and you can write about it in a way a reader ten time zones away will finish.

**That is not a consolation prize. It is expertise, and it is exactly the kind that does not automate, does not commoditise, and does not transfer out of a written portfolio with your name on it.** Take it seriously. Maintain it. And when someone asks you what you did in high school, do not say you took a cybersecurity course — say what you found, what you could not find, and what you would look at next.
