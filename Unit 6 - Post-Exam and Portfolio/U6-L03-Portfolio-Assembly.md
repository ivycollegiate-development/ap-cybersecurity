## PART 1 — WHAT A CYBERSECURITY PORTFOLIO IS FOR (10 min)

Be blunt about this, because the default assumption is wrong.

**A portfolio is not a gallery of screenshots. It is a document that answers one question for a person who has thirty seconds: "can this person actually do the thing, and do they know that they can?"**

That means the reviewer is asking, in order:
1. Can this person *identify* a problem?
2. Can they *reproduce* it?
3. Do they know what the *evidence* is?
4. Can they *explain* what it means?
5. Will they *stand behind* the conclusion?

A screenshot answers none of these. A writeup with a failed attempt, a method, a finding, and a recommendation answers all five. **The failed attempt is the highest-value part and it is the part everyone deletes.** It is the only part that proves you were actually there.

**The two audiences, and they read differently:**

| Audience | What they want first | What loses them |
|----------|--------------------|------------------|
| **Hiring manager / recruiter** (30 seconds) | Evidence you have done real work; a repo with visible history | Wall-to-wall prose, no link to the actual artifact |
| **Technical interviewer** (10 minutes) | Your method, your judgement calls, what you got wrong | Claims with no evidence; tools named but never demonstrated |
| **College application reviewer** | Depth, honesty, sustained interest over time | A portfolio that looks like it was assembled the night before |

**The honest constraint, stated up front:** you are one student, and this course ran a year with a Saturday CTF track. What you have is roughly eight sessions' worth of writeups, a capstone, five GitHub labs with real fix histories, and an exam. That is a genuinely strong portfolio for someone your age. It is not a senior engineer's portfolio, and it does not need to be — the portfolio's job is to show *trajectory and judgement*, not seniority.

## PART 3 — THE STRUCTURE (12 min)

**A portfolio has a shape, and the shape is what makes it readable.** Draft yours today; you will refine it on build days.

**Recommended structure — five parts, and you should resist adding a sixth:**

**1. The one-page front.** Who you are, one line on what you have actually done, the single strongest link, and a contact line. Not a biography. A claim plus evidence. *This is the page a recruiter reads; it must be readable in thirty seconds.*

**2. Three featured pieces.** Not eight. Three, chosen by this test: *which of my artifacts most clearly demonstrates a skill, and which of those is written well enough that a stranger can follow it?* Each gets a short writeup: the problem, what I did, what I found, what I concluded, and what I'd do differently. **The "what I'd do differently" is not optional and not a humblebrag** — it is the sentence that proves you were actually thinking.

**3. The archive.** Everything else, one line each, with links. This is where the other five CTF writeups and the labs live. A list with good titles and honest one-liners is a legitimate and strong section.

**4. The trajectory.** What changed over the year, what you can do now that you could not do in September, and what you are trying to learn next. **This section is what a college application wants and what a hiring manager reads to judge whether you will still be interesting in two years.** It is also the section that makes the portfolio honest rather than impressive.

**5. The writing.** One short piece — a technical blog post, a lab writeup published properly, an explainer of something you found interesting. The point is not the polish; the point is that you can *communicate* technical work in prose. Almost nobody can, and it is the cheapest differentiator available to you.

**One question, then:** does your structure have a **through-line**, or is it a list? If a reader gets a sentence after five minutes that explains *what kind of security person you are becoming* — detection-focused, offensive, governance-leaning, generalist — you have a portfolio. If not, you have a folder.

**The through-line is a sentence you write, and it is a portfolio artifact in its own right.** Draft it today. It will change as the next three weeks add to it, and you will finalise it on May 27.

- ☐ Your archive list — every artifact from the inventory, one line each, with an honest strength rating.

- ☐ Where the portfolio will live. **A public GitHub repository, structured as a repo with a good README, is the right answer** — it is where the work already is, it has visible history, and it is what a technical reviewer expects to click. Decide today.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief.** One current TWNCERT or iThome item, framed as a portfolio question rather than a security question.

**The question:** *could you write this up as a two-page piece a Taiwanese CS student would actually read — and would it be worth reading?*

Because that is the portfolio section 5, and it is the section nobody in this room has done yet. The most valuable thing you can produce in the next three weeks is a **written piece on a local incident, in a form a real technical reader would finish.** A writeup of a TWNCERT advisory. An analysis of a ransomware campaign targeting Taiwanese organisations. A walkthrough of an attack pattern you traced in your own lab. The technical content already exists and the local context already makes it distinctive.

**The specific advantage, and you should say it in your own portfolio:** a candidate who can analyse a Taiwanese incident and explain it to an international audience is doing something a candidate who read the same advisory in an English-language summary cannot. Local threat knowledge is a genuine professional differentiator, not a consolation prize for being in Taiwan. Treat it as an asset in the portfolio, because it is one.
