Homework Quiz Answer Key — Unit 2

U2 L8 - Key: Homework Quiz Physical Vulnerabilities and Attacks (Unit 2)

10 points, 2 per question. The target skill is specificity. A student who writes "the person was tricked" has described an incident, not an attack vector, and earns at most 1. Every answer below names the vector, then names a control that operates by construction rather than by someone's attention.

## Q1. An attacker enters a secure office behind a delivery driver, through a door the driver was opening legitimately. Name the attack and name one control that eliminates it by construction, not by vigilance.

A. Tailgating, also called piggybacking. The control that eliminates it by construction is a mantrap or vestibule — a two-door interlock where the inner door does not release until the outer door closes and occupancy is verified to be one person. It works because there is no moment at which the door is open to two people, so there is no opportunity to exploit, regardless of how determined the person at the front desk is. Also acceptable: reverse tailgating language is not needed here, but credit a student who notes a staffed door is only a partial control because a guard can be distracted, phoned, or reads uniforms and credentials rather than intent. The phrase "by construction" is the whole question — a policy that says "no one follows anyone in" is a written control, not a constructed one, and the student should be able to say why that is a weaker answer.

## Q2. A contractor's 125 kHz proximity badge is photographed and later used on a Sunday when no one is at the desk. Name the attack, and name the credential type that would have made it much harder.

A. Credential cloning, or badge cloning, followed by badge theft and unauthorized entry. 125 kHz legacy proximity cards are clonable because they emit a static, unchanging code with no cryptographic challenge, so a reader that accepts "12345" will accept an attacker-made card transmitting "12345." A smart card using EMV or higher-frequency technology, a mobile credential using NFC with a rotating code, or a biometric bound to a live person all make the copy much harder, because the credential responds to a challenge and is bound to something the attacker does not have. The phrase "when no one is at the desk" is the detective half of the question and is worth the second point if the student says the entry was undetected — this is the case that comes back tomorrow in 2.4 on access logs, and it is the reason a physical badge system generates a detective layer at all.

## Q3. A student in a cafe types a password where the person at the next table can read the screen. Name the attack, and name one physical control that reduces it.

A. Shoulder surfing. Controls that reduce it: a privacy filter on the screen, a screen angled away from public sightlines, moving to a wall or a less visible seat, or simply reducing screen time on the credential itself — the strongest version is not entering a password at a cafe at all, which is what password managers and passkeys enable. The point to credit is that a purely technical control such as a longer password does not help here, because the attacker reads the whole thing regardless of length. Any answer that names a physical or environmental change is correct; an answer that says "use a stronger password" is the wrong kind of control for this attack.

## Q4. The office dumpster is behind an unlocked gate. Name the physical attack this enables, and say what the attacker is looking for.

A. Dumpster diving, also called trash snooping. The target is not trash — it is any document or storage medium that has not been destroyed, including printed meeting notes with credentials or project names, employee lists and org charts that support social engineering, discarded drives or SSDs that were not wiped or shredded, backup media, and anything that reveals which vendor or which system is in use. Unlocked is doing real work in this question: a locked or alarmed enclosure is a control, and its absence is what makes this a physical attack rather than a data-handling failure. Credit the student who links it forward — a name on a whiteboard or a printer queue in a landfill is the entry point for a later social engineering attack, which is the same chain as the RSA SecurID case on Friday.

## Q5. The server closet has a fingerprint reader on the door. Name one documented way an attacker defeats that reader without touching the physical device.

A. The lifted or replicated fingerprint. A gelatin or silicone replica of a target's finger, taken from a glass or a document, is pressed against the reader and is accepted by a device that checks only that a valid ridge pattern is present and not that the finger is attached to a living person. Also acceptable, depending on what the class covered: photographs or images presented to face recognition systems in the absence of liveness detection; a mold or cast taken from a target's finger. Follow up on the configuration question if there is time: many devices can be set to fail open (grant access on sensor error) or fail closed (deny it), and fail open turns any sensor weakness into a bypass — which is a physical configuration decision with a security consequence, and connects straight to next Monday's catalog work on cost and failure modes.
