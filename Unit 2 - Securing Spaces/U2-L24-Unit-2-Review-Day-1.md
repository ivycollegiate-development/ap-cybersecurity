# U2 L24: Unit 2 Review Day 1 (Nov 9, Mon — PAPER DAY)

**⚡ HW Quiz (12 min):** Graded HW quiz on the Fall Break physical security audit. Every hand-written audit handed in today counts. This is the one graded assessment of the break work — there is no second chance and no make-up.

**Learning Objectives:**
- 2.2.A Distinguish tailgating and piggybacking from other entry failures and specify the control that addresses each
- 2.2.B Explain shoulder surfing and the conditions that make it likely
- 2.3.A Evaluate locks and access controls, including cloning and lock picking, and state why a stronger lock is not a layer
- 2.4.B Explain what a video log shows that a badge log does not, and the reverse

**Materials:** Graded HW quiz (printed), anonymized audit summary board, Unit 2 slides, study guide (student-built, printed)

**No computers today.** Paper day — everything is a page you write on.

---

## Activities

1. **Graded HW quiz (12 min):** The Fall Break audit quiz. Ten questions: five on the vulnerability classifications you were asked to identify, three on likelihood and impact, two on treatment selection. Collected at the end — no notes, no packet.

   Scoring note for students: this is a grade, but it is also diagnostic. The three lowest-confidence items for the class determine the focus for the rest of the week.

2. **Audit debrief (14 min):** Anonymize before you speak. No names on the board, ever.
   - **Most common weakness:** tally the findings across the class and name the one that appeared most often. In past years it has been entry-sequence control, not locks — which surprises people, because locks are what they think about.
   - **Strongest finds:** read out three or four of the best findings. A strong finding is specific, has evidence, and has a risk rating with a reason attached. Name what made each one strong, not just the finding.
   - **The two addenda:** report the shared-network risks and the typhoon/flood resilience gaps the class found, in aggregate and unnamed. Ask which single shared-building network risk is the most consequential, and let students argue.
   - **The honest line:** if several audits found the same serious gap in the same kind of building, that is a real finding and I would rather have it raised in class than fixed quietly.

3. **Review Day 1 — attack phases and physical vulnerabilities, driven by the quiz (16 min):** Re-teach only what the quiz showed was weak, using the class's own findings as the examples.
   - **2.1.A–2.1.D attack phases (5 min):** preparatory/reconnaissance, establishing access, lateral movement, exfiltration. Anchor with a physical-to-digital chain: the person who tailgated in during week one is now the one establishing access. Note the phase mapping is the same whether the entry is a badge, a key, or a conversation at a door.
   - **2.2.A tailgating (4 min):** the audit showed how common it is, so this is the one to nail. One badge event, multiple people through. Control is a layer — mantrap, guard, video with a usable viewing angle — not a better card reader.
   - **2.2.B shoulder surfing (3 min):** keypad codes, PIN entry, badge readers with a visible display, keypads in lit doorways. Note that this is why keypad position matters, and why a keypad facing a bright entrance is a design failure.
   - **2.2.C door social engineering, cloning, lock picking (4 min):** the human at the door beats the cylinder every time. Cloning copies a card without touching the lock. Lock picking defeats many locks without a tool trace. Ask which of the three a class's audit would have caught and which it would have missed entirely.

4. **Study guide work time (3 min):** Start or continue the Unit 2 study guide. Minimum before Friday: 2.2 covered, in your own words, with one example per vulnerability that is not from a textbook.

**Homework (due Tue 20:30 — Tuesday quiz):** Finish 2.3 and 2.4 sections of the study guide: locks and access controls, surveillance, environmental controls, badge log monitoring, video/log correlation, alerting thresholds. One sentence per term, in your own words, plus one real example.

**Differentiation / ELL support:** The debrief is the students' own material, which lowers the language load — the vocabulary is already theirs from the audit. Provide a printed classification table for the four attack phases so the review tests matching evidence to phase rather than recall of definitions. For the study guide, supply a term list with the required count per section so students spend their time writing rather than deciding what to include.
