# U2 L10: Physical Security Audit Lab (Oct 13, Tue — PAPER DAY)

**⚡ HW Quiz (5 min):** 4 MCQ on 2.3 protecting physical spaces (lock types and where each fails, badge reader vs. keypad, what CCTV can and cannot tell you).

**Learning Objectives:**
- 2.3.A Evaluate locks and access controls as physical preventive controls, including where each control fails
- 2.3.B Assess surveillance coverage against a stated purpose rather than by camera count
- 2.3.C Identify environmental and monitoring controls (lighting, sightlines, sensors) and rate their effectiveness

**Materials:** Printed audit checklist (one per student), clipboard per student, campus route map, high-visibility vest per group, walk audit summary sheet, no computers

**No computers today.** Paper day — the audit happens on the printed checklist, in the building.

---

## Activities

1. **Review the checklist and safety rules (8 min):** Board work, before anyone moves. Read the safety rules aloud, in full, and have students repeat the two that matter most: stay on public paths, and do not touch anything.

   **Safety rules — non-negotiable:**
   - Stay on public paths and driveways. Do not enter restricted areas, loading docks, or any space behind an access-controlled door.
   - **Do not touch doors, badge readers, keypads, gates, fences, cameras, or windows.** You are observing the control, not testing it.
   - **Do not test access control.** Do not badge someone in, prop a door, follow anyone through a door, or ask staff to let you in somewhere. A tailgating experiment in a real building is a real security incident.
   - The teacher leads every route. Stay with your group, stay in sight of the teacher, and if the route stops, stop.
   - Do not photograph other students, staff, or visitors. Do not write down anyone's name.
   - If you see an actual security problem on the walk, tell the teacher immediately and do not investigate it.

   Then walk through the checklist together: for each control, the rating scale —
   - **Present & Effective** — the control exists and does what it is supposed to do.
   - **Present but Weak** — the control exists but is bypassable, misaligned, degraded, or gamed.
   - **Missing** — no control here, and a reasonable person would expect one.

   The scale is the assessment. "There's a camera, so it's fine" is not a rating — cameras have placement, blind spots, lighting, and retention. Push on that in the debrief.

2. **Campus walk audit (20 min):** Groups of 4 with a teacher-led route. Three route segments, roughly six minutes each:
   - **Route A — Main entry sequence:** approach path, front doors, visitor process, reception desk sightlines.
   - **Route B — Interior circulation:** corridor junctions, stairwells, roof access, emergency exits, where badges are required vs. assumed.
   - **Route C — Perimeter and parking:** fence line, bike area, delivery point, dumpster and loading zone, after-hours exterior lighting.

   Each student fills their own checklist and rates independently. Groups then reconcile — the disagreements are the interesting part, and the debrief needs them.

   Record for every rated item: the location, the rating, and **one sentence of evidence**. "Missing" without evidence is an opinion; "Missing: no reader on the roof access door, padlock on exterior" is a finding.

3. **Debrief (12 min):** Each group reports their three worst findings and their three strongest controls. Build a shared master list on the board, grouped by *category* rather than by location — the pattern across a building is the finding, not any single door.

   Questions to force the analysis past observation:
   - Where is the cheapest control that fixes the most findings?
   - Which of your "Present but Weak" items is weak because of a *design* flaw, and which is weak because of a *human process* (a propped door, a shared code, a badge left on a lanyard)?
   - Name one control you rated that an attacker could defeat by walking in through a different door. Is it a real control, or decoration?

4. **Write 3 prioritized recommendations (5 min):** Individually, on the summary sheet. For each: the control to add or change, the specific finding it fixes, and its priority. Priority must be justified by likelihood and impact — the same two axes from Thursday's risk matrix.

   This is the deliverable and it feeds the Unit 2 test FRQ, which routinely asks students to recommend a control and defend the priority.

**Homework (due Wed 20:30 — Thursday quiz):** Read CED 2.4 (detecting physical attacks) and be ready to discuss: prevention found the unlocked door, but how would anyone have *known* it was unlocked? Bring one question about monitoring to class.

**Differentiation / ELL support:** The checklist is pre-sectioned by control type with the scale definitions printed at the top, so the rating decision is the work rather than the form. Students who need narrower scope ride the route with a sub-checklist for one segment only. A written sentence frame for the evidence field and the priority justification is available. The findings are concrete and visual, which makes the debrief discussion accessible to all students — let them report a finding they observed rather than a general impression.
