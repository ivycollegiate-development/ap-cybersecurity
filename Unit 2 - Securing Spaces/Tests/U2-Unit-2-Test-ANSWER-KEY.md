## Section I — Question-by-question rationale

### 1. A — 2.1.A (preparatory / reconnaissance)

Photographing badge readers, counting arrivals, and noting unattended hours is collection before any entry. No access has been gained and nothing has been taken.

- **B** would require the intruder to be inside.
- **C** would require movement from an initial foothold.
- **D** would require data or assets leaving the site.

### 2. B — 2.1.B (establishing access)

The duplicate badge opens the door and the intruder steps inside for the first time. That is the moment access is established.

- **A** is the collection that preceded it.
- **C** is movement after access.
- **D** is removal of assets.

### 3. C — 2.1.C (lateral movement)

The intruder is already inside and moves to a second, more valuable location — the key cabinet and then the records room. Movement from one internal position to another is lateral movement.

- **A** happened before entry.
- **B** was the original entry.
- **D** would be removal of data or assets, not internal repositioning.

### 4. A — 2.1.E (ALE / ARO arithmetic)

ALE = SLE × ARO, so ARO = ALE ÷ SLE = NT$1,500,000 ÷ NT$6,000,000 = **0.25**.

- **B** inverts the formula (SLE ÷ ALE).
- **C** and **D** are arithmetic distractors with no basis in the stated figures.

### 5. B — 2.1.E (inherent vs residual)

High/High is the **inherent** risk — the rating before controls. After the equipment is moved to the third floor, the **residual** risk is Medium/Medium. Moving assets out of the flood zone reduces both the chance floodwater reaches them (likelihood) and the loss if it does (impact).

- **A** reverses inherent and residual.
- **C** is the tempting near-miss: the control did more than reduce likelihood alone.
- **D** is wrong — no control produces zero residual risk.

### 6. D — 2.1.E (cost-benefit justification)

Annual reduction in expected loss = NT$2,400,000 − NT$600,000 = **NT$1,800,000**, which exceeds the NT$900,000 annual cost. The control is justified.

- **A** applies the wrong test — controls rarely remove risk entirely, and that is not the justification standard.
- **B** compares the wrong figures (residual ALE to cost, rather than reduction to cost).
- **C** rejects on absolute cost without comparing it to the loss it prevents.

### 7. C — 2.1.F (risk avoidance)

Stopping the practice of storing scanned passports removes the activity that creates the risk. That is avoidance.

- **A** keeps the activity and adds controls — mitigation.
- **B** moves the financial consequence to an insurer — transfer.
- **D** documents a decision to take no action — acceptance.

### 8. D — 2.1.F (risk transfer)

A policy that pays for forced-entry losses moves the financial consequence to a third party. That is transfer.

- **A** and **B** reduce likelihood or impact while keeping the activity — mitigation.
- **C** is documentation with no action — acceptance.

### 9. C — 2.1.D (exfiltration)

The intruder is already inside the records room and carries backup drives out through the loading dock. Removal of assets from the site is exfiltration.

- **A** happened before entry.
- **B** was the original entry.
- **D** was movement to the records room, which preceded this.

### 10. D — 2.1.F (least appropriate treatment)

The alarm costs NT$40,000 once and cuts ALE by NT$900,000 (NT$1,200,000 → NT$300,000). The reduction is more than twenty times the cost. **Accepting** the risk while an inexpensive, effective control exists is the least appropriate choice.

- **A**, **B**, and **C** are all defensible treatments for a cheap, effective control.
- **D** leaves an avoidable, expensive exposure in place.

### 11. B — 2.2.A (tailgating / piggybacking)

Four people pass on one badge read and the log records one event. One credential admitted multiple people — tailgating.

- **A** is observation of a code or credential, not following through a door.
- **C** would produce a second valid read, not a single read for four people.
- **D** leaves physical damage or manipulation evidence and is unrelated to the count mismatch.

### 12. A — 2.2.B (shoulder surfing)

The visitor observes the PIN being typed and reuses it. Observation of a credential in use is shoulder surfing.

- **B** is following someone through a door without their own credential.
- **C** involves deceiving a person into granting access, not watching a code.
- **D** is copying a credential's data, not reading a typed PIN.

### 13. C — 2.2.C (door social engineering — pretexting / impersonation)

The intruder invents a role, claims an urgent purpose, and relies on the staff member skipping verification. That is social engineering at the door.

- **A** is following through on someone else's credential.
- **B** is observing a code or credential.
- **D** leaves physical evidence on the lock.

### 14. C — 2.3.A (cryptographic authentication defeats cloning)

A copied identifier is useless if the door requires a cryptographic challenge-response the copy cannot produce. This is the control that defeats the duplicate specifically.

- **A** helps a human spot a copied badge but does not stop the copy from working at the reader.
- **B** changes the PIN, which is not the cloned credential.
- **D** detects use after the fact; it does not prevent the duplicate from opening the door.

### 15. B — 2.3.A (prevention vs detection)

A mantrap or single-person interlock physically admits one person at a time. That prevents tailgating.

- **A**, **C**, and **D** are detection or documentation controls — they produce a record after the event.

### 16. A — 2.3.B (coverage by placement and field of view)

Camera count is not coverage. A camera is only useful if its field of view captures the approach path and the door face. Twelve badly placed cameras leave the closet unobserved.

- **B** invents a minimum camera count that the CED does not set.
- **C** is a real limitation but does not explain why twelve cameras miss one door.
- **D** is an overgeneralisation — video and logs answer different questions.

### 17. A — 2.3.C (environmental controls)

Flood risk to a ground-floor closet is addressed by raising the equipment above the expected flood level, detecting water with an alerting sensor, and giving water a secondary path out (drainage or backflow valve).

- **B** addresses power continuity and cooling, not flooding.
- **C** addresses access, not water.
- **D** addresses unauthorised entry, not water.

### 18. D — 2.4.B (what a video trigger proves)

A line-crossing trigger proves something crossed a defined line in the camera's field of view. It does not identify the person, and it does not confirm the trigger was a person at all.

- **A** overstates — the frame notes say the face is not identifiable.
- **B** is not established by a motion trigger.
- **C** overstates — a trigger is an exception, not proof of intrusion.

### 19. B — 2.4.A / 2.4.B (correlating logs and video)

The closet has no card reader, so a key or a picked lock leaves **no badge record** — the absence of a read is expected, not proof the door stayed shut. The person seen at 02:19 may have entered earlier through a door left unlatched. Both explanations fit the records without requiring the camera or the log to be wrong.

- **A** invents two system failures with no evidence.
- **C** misreads what a badge log records — it records credential reads, not door status.
- **D** is false — different systems can and do disagree about the same minute.

### 20. D — 2.4.C (alerting threshold)

With 400 events a night and three staff, alerting on everything is noise and alerting on nothing is negligence. A threshold that combines a failed-then-successful read at the same reader within two minutes, plus any read in a zone with no scheduled activity, targets the events that actually indicate an attack.

- **A** and **C** generate more alerts than three people can triage, so real alerts get missed.
- **B** guarantees the 02:19-type event is never seen until the next morning.

## Section II — Free Response (10 points)

### Model answer

**(a) Vulnerabilities — 2 points (1 per vulnerability, with CED label)**

1. **The closet door has no card reader and only a standard knob lock (2.2.C — lock picking / physical bypass).** A standard interior knob lock can be picked, bumped, or bypassed with a shim, and because there is no reader, the entry produces no credential event. The 02:19 sensor record shows the door opened with no badge event at all.

2. **One badge read can admit multiple people at the back-of-house door (2.2.A — tailgating).** The log shows B-114 entering at 02:07 and then being denied at 02:11 as "already inside," while B-201 enters at 02:12. A single badge event for a door used by a shift change is consistent with people following through behind a badge holder. The camera's 02:12 line-crossing shows one person with a hood up — a single face is not established.

*Also creditable:* the camera covers the restroom corridor but not the closet door (2.3.B surveillance gap) — accept as a vulnerability if labelled 2.2/2.3 correctly.

**(b) Controls — 2 points (1 per vulnerability)**

1. **For the closet door:** replace the knob with a restricted-keyway lockset **and** put the closet on the access-control system with a card reader and a door-position alarm that annunciates (2.3.A). The restricted keyway defeats picking and key duplication; the reader creates a credential record where none exists today; the alarm converts a silent door-open into an event someone must answer.

2. **For the back-of-house door:** install a mantrap or single-person interlock with anti-tailgating sensors (2.3.A), **or** — cheaper — a door-position sensor plus a camera whose field of view captures the approach path and the door face together (2.3.B), paired with an alert when one credential is followed by a second door-position event within a short window. The first prevents; the second detects and creates the count record the log cannot produce.

**(c) Treatment — 2 points (1 per finding, justified against cost and impact)**

1. **Closet door: mitigate.** The lockset and alarm cost NT$35,000 against equipment and outage valued at NT$1,200,000. A single prevented loss pays for the control roughly 34 times over. Mitigation reduces both the likelihood of bypass and the impact of a successful entry. *Accept "mitigate, then insure the residual" only with a stated reason.*

2. **Back-of-house door: mitigate.** The same cost scale applies; the control is inexpensive relative to the exposure. *Accept "avoid" only if the student argues the back-of-house entry should be eliminated or relocated — but they must say so explicitly and justify it against operational need.*

**(d) The 02:19 event — 2 points**

**What the records prove:** the badge log proves which **credentials** were presented and accepted at the back-of-house door. The closet contact sensor proves the closet door **opened** at 02:19. Together they prove the closet door was opened at a time when no badge event occurred within five minutes — that is, the entry left **no credential record**.

**What they cannot prove:** neither record identifies **who** opened the closet door, and neither proves the door was locked beforehand. The absence of a badge read does not prove the door stayed shut — the closet has no reader, so a key or a picked lock would leave exactly this record. The badge log also cannot prove how many people passed on a single read; it records credentials, not people.

**Correlation with the camera:** the 02:12 line-crossing shows one hooded person at the front desk counter; the 02:19 closet sensor fires seven minutes later. The camera does not cover the closet door, so it cannot show the person entering — but the timing is consistent with someone moving from the lobby toward the closet.

**Two plausible explanations for the 02:19 door-open with no badge event:**
1. **Key or picked lock.** The closet has no card reader, so anyone with a key — or anyone who picks the knob lock — opens it without generating a badge event.
2. **Earlier unlatched entry.** Someone entered through a door left unlatched or propped earlier and was already inside; the 02:19 sensor records their movement, not their entry.

**(e) Alerting — 2 points**

**Threshold and response:** *"Any closet-door open event with no matching badge read in the preceding five minutes triggers an immediate alert to the on-duty security lead, who must physically check the closet within ten minutes and log the outcome."*

Accept variants that name all four elements: **threshold** (closet open with no matching badge read in a defined window), **action** (alert and physical check), **owner** (on-duty security lead / named role), **time bound** (within ten minutes, or a comparable explicit bound).

**Why the current approach fails 2.4.C:** emailing exceptions to the facilities manager, who reads them the next morning, produces a record with **no time bound and no owner on shift** — the alert arrives after the loss window has closed, which is detection without response.
