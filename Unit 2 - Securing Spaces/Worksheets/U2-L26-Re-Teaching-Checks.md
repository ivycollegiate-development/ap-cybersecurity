# U2 L26 — Re-Teaching Checks (Nov 11)

**Teacher copy — includes answers. Do not distribute as-is.**

Use the three items under whichever sprint objective the class missed most. Each set takes about four minutes: read the item aloud, students answer on paper or out loud, then re-teach from the answer note before moving on. One set per re-teach cycle; do not run all five sets in one period.

The Nov 11 sprint covers five objectives. Score the sprint, find the weakest, then open that objective's set below.

---

## Objective 2.1.A — Preparatory / reconnaissance (sprint Q1)

**Check 1.** A person walks the perimeter of a building twice over two days, photographs the loading dock, and leaves. Has an attack occurred? Name the phase.

*Answer note:* No attack yet — nothing was accessed or taken. The activity is preparatory/reconnaissance: collection that enables a later phase. Re-teach point: **reconnaissance is a phase, not a crime** — it is the step that makes the later steps possible. Ask: "What would have to happen next for this to become establishing access?"

**Check 2.** An intruder watches staff arrive for a week to learn shift times, then uses a copied badge to enter. Which phase did the week of watching belong to, and which phase was the badge entry?

*Answer note:* Watching = 2.1.A preparatory/reconnaissance. Badge entry = 2.1.B establishing access. Re-teach point: the phases are **sequential and distinct**; the same person occupies different phases at different times. Ask students to draw the timeline and label each segment.

**Check 3.** A student says, "Photographing the badge reader is establishing access because they're at the door." What is wrong with that statement?

*Answer note:* Being at the door is not access. Establishing access requires **getting through** a control — a credential accepted, a lock bypassed, a person talked past the door. Photographing a reader is still collection. Re-teach point: the test for 2.1.B is **whether a barrier was crossed**, not whether the intruder was near it.

## Objective 2.1.E / 2.1.F — Risk assessment and treatment (sprint Q2)

**Check 1.** ALE is NT$2,400,000. A control costing NT$900,000 per year reduces ALE to NT$600,000. Justify or reject the control in one sentence using the numbers.

*Answer note:* Justified — the annual reduction is NT$1,800,000, double the NT$900,000 cost. Re-teach point: the comparison is **reduction in ALE vs. annual cost**, never "residual ALE vs. cost" and never "cost is large." Make students say both numbers out loud.

**Check 2.** Management stops storing scanned passports on site. Name the treatment and say why it is not mitigation.

*Answer note:* Avoidance — the activity that creates the risk is eliminated. Mitigation would keep the activity and reduce likelihood or impact. Re-teach point: **avoidance removes the source; mitigation manages the risk.** If the activity still happens, it is not avoidance.

**Check 3.** A policy pays for forced-entry losses. A student calls this mitigation because "the loss is smaller now." Correct the student.

*Answer note:* It is transfer. The financial consequence moves to the insurer; the likelihood and the operational impact of the event do not change — the assets are still gone. Re-teach point: **insurance moves money, not risk.** The event still happens at the same rate and does the same damage.

## Objective 2.2.C — Door social engineering (sprint Q3)

**Check 1.** A person in a courier uniform with a clipboard says, "Delivery for the third floor, I need to bring it up," and staff hold the door. Name the attack and the specific failure.

*Answer note:* Social engineering at the door — pretexting/impersonation (2.2.C). The failure is that staff granted access on a **claimed role and urgency** without verifying a work order or calling the recipient. Re-teach point: the attack works because **the request sounds routine**, not because the uniform is convincing. Ask what single check would have stopped it.

**Check 2.** How is this different from tailgating?

*Answer note:* Tailgating follows an authorised person through on their credential. Social engineering gets an authorised person to **grant** access by deception — the intruder may never touch the door. Re-teach point: tailgating uses someone's **credential**; social engineering uses someone's **decision**.

**Check 3.** A student says the defence is "train staff to be suspicious of everyone." Why is that insufficient?

*Answer note:* Suspicion is not a procedure. The defence is a **verification step** — check the work order, call the recipient, require escort — applied to every request regardless of how routine it looks. Re-teach point: controls must be **procedural and consistent**, not dependent on a staff member's mood or judgement.

## Objective 2.3.A–2.3.C — Physical protections (sprint Q4)

**Check 1.** A building adds a stronger lock to the server room door. A student says the room is now layered. Correct the student.

*Answer note:* A stronger lock is still **one control of one type**. Layering means independent controls of different types — a lock plus surveillance plus an alarm plus a procedure. A stronger version of the same control does not add a layer. Re-teach point: **layers are different kinds of control**, not more of the same kind.

**Check 2.** A camera is mounted above a door, pointing down at the top of people's heads. What is wrong, and what is the fix?

*Answer note:* The field of view captures heads, not the **approach path and the door face**. Coverage is judged by what the frame shows, not by whether a camera exists. Fix: reposition so the frame captures who approaches and who passes through. Re-teach point: 2.3.B is about **placement and viewing angle**, not camera count.

**Check 3.** A ground-floor network closet floods in typhoon season. Name three controls that address the water specifically.

*Answer note:* Raise the equipment above the expected flood level; install a water-leak sensor with an alert; add secondary drainage or a backflow valve. Re-teach point: **environmental controls address the environment**, not access. A camera or a lock does nothing about water.

## Objective 2.4.B — Correlating video with logs (sprint Q5)

**Check 1.** A badge log shows one read at 02:19. A camera shows two people entering at 02:19. What do the two records together establish?

*Answer note:* One credential event and two people — the second person produced no badge event. That is consistent with tailgating. Re-teach point: **the camera counts people; the log counts credentials.** The mismatch is the evidence. Do not overclaim which door they used unless the record shows it.

**Check 2.** A student says the camera "proves" the second person used a cloned badge. Why is that unsupported?

*Answer note:* A cloned badge would generate a **second read**. The log shows one read. The absence of a second read argues against cloning and toward following through. Re-teach point: **match the proposed cause to the record it would produce**; if the record does not show that signature, the cause is unsupported.

**Check 3.** A line-crossing trigger fires on the loading-dock camera at 23:40. Name two things the trigger proves and one thing it does not.

*Answer note:* Proves something crossed a defined line in the field of view at 23:40, and that the camera system was recording. Does **not** prove the trigger was a person, identify who it was, or prove the dock was locked. Re-teach point: **a trigger is an exception, not an identification.**
