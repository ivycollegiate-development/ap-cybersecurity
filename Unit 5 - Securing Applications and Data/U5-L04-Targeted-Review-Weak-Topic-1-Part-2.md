## PART 1 — COLD CLOSE-OUT ON THE GAP LIST (16 min)

Notes and study guides **closed and face down**. On the desk: yesterday's concept map, yesterday's numbered gap list, and a clean sheet. Nothing else.

**We work the gap list top to bottom.** For each item on it, I call on a student — cold, no warning, no notes. Two-minute answer.

Then the same item goes to the whole room in writing, ninety seconds, before we hear the answers. Writing first, hearing second. The order matters: hearing an answer you did not produce yourself feels like learning and is not.

Then we compare three things per gap:
- ☐ Did you produce the specific term? Not the category — the term.
- ☐ Could you connect it to the artifact it appears in?
- ☐ Could you say what the *opposite* or *near-miss* concept is, and why a test item might offer it as a distractor?

**That third bullet is where the remaining points are.** Most exam distractors are not random wrong answers — they are *nearly correct* concepts. Someone who knows a term cold but cannot name its near-miss still gets caught.

**The scoring rule for today:** a gap closes when **two different people** produce the answer cold. If only you can produce it, it is not closed, it is memorised by one person, and it goes back on the list.

## PART 2 — ARTIFACT READING UNDER TIME (16 min)

Every AP item in this course that is worth points shows you an artifact. Today, weak topic 1's artifacts, timed.

**Stations.** I project or hand out artifacts of the type most common for this topic — for example, for a network topic: a config excerpt, a routing table, a log block, an architecture diagram; for an app/data topic: a vulnerable code snippet, a server log, a permission string, a certificate chain; for a device topic: a hardening checklist, a registry setting, an EDR alert. **You will not know which artifacts until you see them, so read the artifact first, then decide which topic you are in.** That ordering is itself a scored skill.

**Per artifact, eight minutes:**

1. **(2 min) What am I looking at?** Name the artifact type and the technology. Out loud or on paper — do not skip to analysis.
2. **(3 min) What is normal here?** Establish the baseline. Every artifact has a normal state, and detection questions are always relative to it.
3. **(2 min) What is wrong, and how do I know?** Cite the *specific field or line* that is the evidence, not a general impression. "Line 14 contains a quote character in a query parameter" earns; "it looks hacky" does not.
4. **(1 min) What do I do about it?** One specific control.

**The standard:** a response that names an artifact field earns the point. A response that describes a feeling does not.

## PART 3 — COLD EXIT CHECK (9 min)

Six questions on slips. **No notes, no discussion, three minutes each, silent.** These are the items I will use to set Monday's re-teach priorities, so answer the way you would on an exam — not the way you would if you could think for ten more minutes.

Write, on the bottom of your slip, one line:

> *The single thing I would still be nervous about being asked on May 5 is ______.*

That line is the most important thing on the sheet, and I read every one of them on Saturday.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief.** Take one artifact from the stations — whatever the topic produced — and read it as if it came from a Taiwanese organisation rather than a generic one.

**The exam-shaped question:** *does anything in this artifact change your answer, and why?*

Realistic answers, all acceptable if you can defend them: a device with a management interface exposed on a building network is worse in a multi-tenant apartment building in Taipei than in a single-tenant house; a data store in a fab has a physical-access constraint that a cloud store does not; cross-border transfer of personal data out of Taiwan invokes obligations that a purely domestic scenario does not.

**The transferable point, and the one that earns points:** on the exam, a scenario detail is usually there for a reason. Identify *why the detail is there* and answer the question that the detail creates. Students who answer the question they wish had been asked lose the point the detail was written to earn.
