## PART 1 — HOW A SWEEP WORKS (4 min)

Rules, and they are strict, because a sweep that stops to re-teach stops being a sweep:

1. **Answer, do not discuss.** On paper, alone, committed.
2. **Six minutes per block. Called hard.** Being mid-sentence does not extend it.
3. **A blank is a finding, and it is the most valuable thing on the sheet.** A blank says the topic is not there at all, which is a completely different problem from being unsure — and it tells you where to spend the last week.
4. **Mark confidence, not just correctness.** Next to each answer: `sure` / `guess` / `blank`. A correct guess is a gap you do not know you have, and it will cost you on a harder item.

## PART 2 — UNIT 1 SWEEP (13 min)

**Unit 1 — Introduction to Security.** Sweep items cover: the attacker's objectives and how they differ; the phases of a cyberattack; adversary categories; risk assessment vocabulary (likelihood, impact, inherent, residual); risk treatment options (avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept) and when each is right; social engineering and human factors; AI on both the offensive and the defensive side.

**Watch for, and I will ask cold:**
- You give a risk *treatment* and I ask for a risk *control* — say which one you just produced.
- You name inherent and residual risk backwards. Inherent is **before** the control. Residual is **after**. Say the sentence out loud until it is automatic.
- An item gives a scenario and you reach for the attacker's *motivation* when it asked for the *technique*.

## PART 3 — UNIT 2 SWEEP (13 min)

**Unit 2 — Securing Spaces.** Physical vulnerability and attack types; physical controls and why they matter to a security programme; video surveillance and access logs as an evidence source; badge and access-log analysis; correlating sensor and log data; resilience — power, environmental, and continuity.

**Watch for:**
- You are given a badge log and asked about *behaviour* and you describe the *hardware*. Badges tell you a story; the reader infers the behaviour.
- You are asked what a log can *prove* and you answer what it *suggests*. This is the Thursday lesson in a new place, and it is worth points every time.
- An item asks for the physical control and you give an administrative one, or the reverse. Name the layer.

## PART 4 — UNIT 3 SWEEP (13 min)

**Unit 3 — Securing Networks.** Network attack vectors; reconnaissance versus exploitation; segmentation (VLANs, subnets, DMZ, zero trust); firewall types, rule sets, and order-of-evaluation reasoning; wireless security; IDS versus IPS and the NIDS/NIPS distinction; SIEM correlation; and detection of scanning versus authorised scanning.

**Watch for:**
- NIDS versus NIPS. Say the verb: a NIDS **detects and alerts**; a NIPS sits **inline and blocks**. The verb is the whole distinction and it is a free point you either take or drop.
- Signature-based versus anomaly-based detection, and the trade-off: signatures catch known things precisely, anomaly catches the unknown at the cost of false positives. If an item gives a novel attack with no signature, anomaly is the answer — and the false-positive cost is the follow-up.
- A rule-ordering question. Firewall rule evaluation is ordered; students assume it is not. Work the rules in order on the board if you need to.

## PART 5 — COLD TRIAGE (5 min)

Read your own sheet. Build the triage in three lines:

- ☐ **Three topics where I went blank.** These are the worst; the sweep's whole value is this line.
- ☐ **Two topics where I was guessing.** Nearly as bad.
- ☐ **Two topics where I was sure and right.** These need no further work — and naming them matters, because a student who cannot identify what they already know cannot protect it in the last three days.

**Keep this sheet. Monday's sweep and Tuesday's final review both read from it.**

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief, one per unit.** Three short Taiwan items, one for each unit swept today, in the same "what does the local detail change" form we have used all week.

- **Unit 1:** a phishing wave targeting a Taiwanese organisation. What in the local context changes the human-factors analysis? (Acceptable: multilingual and locally-fluent social engineering, brand impersonation of local institutions, SMS-based lures against a high mobile-penetration population.)
- **Unit 2:** a Taiwanese fab's cleanroom and the physical perimeter around it. What does a local physical-security analysis have to consider that a generic warehouse does not?
- **Unit 3:** a building-level network in a multi-tenant apartment block — one flat network, an unmanaged switch, a management interface reachable from tenant Wi-Fi. Which Unit 3 concept is violated, and what is the one change that fixes the most?

**The recurring point across all three, and it is the transferable one:** the CED vocabulary is the same everywhere. What the local scenario does is make one answer clearly more defensible than another. On the exam, the scenario detail is there to disambiguate — read it as a clue about which concept is being tested.
