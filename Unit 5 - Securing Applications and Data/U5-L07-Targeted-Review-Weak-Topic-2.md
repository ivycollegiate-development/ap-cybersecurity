## PART 2 — THE TOPIC, FAST AND STRUCTURAL (14 min)

**Weak topic 2: _______________**

Cold, from memory, no notes on the desk:

1. **You say the structure.** I write it, you check me. Every topic in this course has a shape — categories, a decision procedure, or a comparison table. The shape is the skeleton the terms hang on, and if you have the shape, missing one term is recoverable; if you only have terms, a missing one leaves a hole.
2. **You fill in the terms against the structure.** I give the category; you name what belongs in it, from memory. Wrong answers are the point — they localise the hole.
3. **You name the near-misses.** For each major term: what is the *almost* the same concept that an item would offer as a distractor, and what single fact separates them? This is the highest-value five minutes in the block. A student who can say "ACLs gate access; encryption protects content; they are not alternatives, they compose" does not get caught by the distractor.
4. **You state the vocabulary the exam rewards.** The CED's phrasing, not your notes' phrasing. When an item and an answer use different words for the same idea, the *item's* word is the one that counts.

## PART 3 — THE ARTIFACT (16 min)

**This is where the points are, per your data.** Four artifacts of the type most common for topic 2, twelve minutes each. Structure per artifact:

1. **(3 min) Read it straight through without analysing.** No notes. Get the shape of it.
2. **(3 min) What is this artifact and what technology is it from?** Name the artifact type. If you cannot name it, stop and say so — naming it wrong means analysing it wrong, and a wrong frame produces confident wrong answers.
3. **(4 min) What is normal here, and what is the deviation?** Establish the baseline explicitly. Half of all misreads come from analysing an artifact that was never abnormal.
4. **(2 min) Name the finding, cite the exact field or line as evidence.** Not "the permissions look wrong" — which character, which line, which value.

**Hard rule, and I will check it on every artifact:** the evidence citation is mandatory. A finding without a cited field scores partial credit at best. This is exactly the habit the Wednesday Artifacts practice in Unit 4 already taught you; the exam version just does not tell you which field matters.

**Finish each artifact with a one-line remediation** naming a specific control. Even on a multiple-choice item, writing the remediation first is how you avoid being tempted by the appealing wrong answer.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief, in artifact form.** Take one of today's four artifacts and re-read it as belonging to a Taiwanese organisation.

**Ask (2 min):** *does the local context change any of your four findings, and which one?*

Worked example of the shape of the answer: if the artifact is a firewall rule set, a rule that is merely permissive in a generic office can be a genuine finding in a Taiwanese fab, because the network it protects is a process-control network where a permissive rule reaches production equipment, not just user laptops. If the artifact is a data store, personal data held in it triggers PIPL obligations on export or on breach that a purely domestic exercise would not raise. **Say the local reason, not just "Taiwan is targeted."**

**Why this earns points:** the exam's scenario details are placed deliberately. "A semiconductor fab," "a hospital," "a school" are not decoration — each one changes which answer is correct, because each implies a different consequence for the same control failure. Students who answer the generic question lose the point the detail was written to earn.
