## PART 1 — FRAMING: THE SCORE IS A MAP, NOT A VERDICT (5 min)

No quiz today. Open your practice exam and your key.

Say the framing out loud: **a practice exam taken at home, self-keyed, over Spring Break, tells you almost nothing about exam-day performance** — no time pressure, notes nearby, a key in your hand. So the number itself is nearly useless. What is *not* useless is the **shape** of the number: which units lost points, which question types lost points, and whether the losses were knowledge gaps or attention errors.

That distinction is the entire lesson. A knowledge gap is closed by studying. An attention error is closed by a habit, not by more studying. Most students spend the next two weeks studying when the real problem was reading the prompt.

Write this on the board and leave it up all block:

> **Missed it because I didn't know it → study it.**
> **Missed it because I didn't read it carefully → practice reading it.**

## PART 2 — BUILD THE CLASS DISTRIBUTION (12 min)

On the board (or a shared sheet), we build three distributions. I will plot the numbers; you keep your own exam face-up so you can place yourself.

**Distribution A — by unit.** Buckets: Unit 1 (Intro/SOC), Unit 2 (Spaces), Unit 3 (Networks), Unit 4 (Devices), Unit 5 (Apps & Data), plus "mixed/cross-unit."

**Distribution B — by item type.** Every item is one of:
- Concept recall (a term, a definition, a "which of these is…")
- Scenario analysis (a described situation, choose the best response)
- Artifact interpretation (a log line, a permission string, a config, a piece of code, a diagram)
- Multi-part response (several linked sub-questions)

**Distribution C — error reason.** For each item you missed, mark the reason honestly:
- `K` — I did not know the concept
- `R` — I misread what was being asked
- `E` — I read it right, reasoned wrong (a genuine misconception)
- `X` — I ran out of time or blanked

Count your own K's. Most students discover they have fewer K's and more E's than they feared — and E is the best category to fix, because an E is a specific, nameable belief about how something works.

**Say the standard before the data goes up:** an honest error-reason tag is worth more than a high score. A student who mislabels every miss as `K` will study the wrong things for eleven days.

## PART 3 — THE THREE WEAK-TOPIC SUMMARIES (16 min)

You submitted three weak-topic summaries over Spring Break. Put them side by side on the desk in the order you ranked them.

We go through them together. For each summary, the class answers four questions out loud:

1. **What did you say was weak?** (Read your own claim.)
2. **Is the practice exam consistent with that claim?** Sometimes a self-assessed weakness never actually shows in the data. That is a real and pleasant finding — you do not need to re-study it.
3. **What kind of weakness is it?** Knowledge (`K`) or reasoning (`E`)? The three-weak-topics summaries are where most students are least honest. This is the day to fix that.
4. **What is the one specific thing that would close it?** Not "review unit 3" — something like "I could not tell NIDS from NIPS under time pressure" or "I keep choosing the more secure-sounding option when the scenario asks for the cheapest one."

I record the three confirmed weak topics on the re-teach board, in order. **That board is the map for the next ten days.** Thursday and Friday are built around topic 1; next Wednesday around topic 2.

## PART 4 — BUILD YOUR OWN REMEDIATION CARD (8 min)

One card, one side, in your own handwriting. Not a study guide — a card you can hold in your head.

| Line | What goes on it |
|------|-----------------|
| **My single weakest topic** | Unit number, topic name, and the CED topic number |
| **What exactly I got wrong** | One sentence. A behavior, not a topic. "I confuse inherent and residual risk." |
| **The evidence** | Which items on my practice exam prove it |
| **The fix** | One concrete action: re-read this section, redraw this diagram, run this lab step again, ask about this in class |
| **How I'll know it worked** | The practice exam item type I will now get right |

The last line is the one that matters. "I'll study more" is not a measurement. "I'll stop missing artifact-interpretation items about permission strings" is.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**Taiwan Threat Brief (3 min opener — first of the block).** Every unit lesson this year opened with a 3-minute Taiwan threat brief. The review block gets the same, but pointed at a different question: **not "what is the current threat" but "which of our own topics would hurt us most."**

Brief: pick the most recent TWNCERT or iThome advisory you have saved from this year and read it against your weak-topic card. Taiwanese organisations are attacked with phishing, ransomware, and supply-chain targeting against the semiconductor sector — and the defenses against all three live in units you have already studied. A phishing email is Unit 1 and Unit 2 (human factors, physical badge and access logs). Ransomware is Unit 4 (hardening, EDR) and Unit 5 (patching, backups, segmentation). Semiconductor supply-chain compromise is the capstone.

**Discussion (2 min):** name one topic on your remediation card where a Taiwanese organisation would be *more* exposed than a generic Western one, and say why in one sentence. The best answers connect to semiconductor concentration, cross-strait targeting, or a genuinely local logistics pattern. There is no single right answer and I am not looking for one.
