## PART 1 — RE-TEACH: THE TOPIC FROM THE GROUND UP (18 min)

**Weak topic 1: _______________** (from the Monday re-teach board)

We do not "review." Review means skimming what you already half-know and feeling fine. We **re-teach from first principles**, which means: start from the definition of the underlying idea, build up, and check comprehension at each step. It is faster than you expect and it exposes the gaps that skimming preserved.

**Method, used all block:**

1. **State the definition cold.** I say the term and its CED definition. You write it in your own words without looking. Now compare. The delta between your version and mine is gap one.
2. **Build the structure.** Every topic in this course has a structure — a set of categories, a decision procedure, a comparison. We draw it on the board from memory, in the CED's vocabulary, not in your notes' vocabulary.
3. **Test it cold.** I ask questions; you answer from the board diagram, not from your paper. Wrong answers are useful. They localise the gap.
4. **Attach the artifact.** Almost every AP item about a topic shows you *something* — a log line, a config, a permission string, a diagram, a scenario. We take the topic's most common artifact types and read one together, line by line, saying out loud what each field is telling us.

**Participation is the grading criterion today.** If you sit silent, you get a silent score, and the whole point of a targeted day is that the targeting is based on what you can and cannot produce on demand. Hand up at least twice, whether or not you are sure.

**Keep a running gap list in the margin of your notes.** Anything you cannot produce from memory goes on the list, verbatim. Friday is spent closing that list.

## PART 2 — CONCEPT MAP FROM MEMORY (14 min)

Close your notes. On a clean sheet, build a concept map of weak topic 1 **from memory only.** Rules:

- **Nodes, not sentences.** Concepts and terms only.
- **Edges must be labeled.** A line that says "leads to" is a decoration. A line that says "is detected by" or "is mitigated by" is a point.
- **Every CED sub-topic in the topic appears as a node.** If you cannot remember what the sub-topics are, that is itself a finding — write `GAP: don't know the sub-topics` and keep going.
- **Cross-links out of the topic.** Weak topic 1 is never isolated. Draw at least two lines to another unit — the risk vocabulary from Unit 2, the logging vocabulary from Unit 3 or 4, the laws from Unit 5. A topic you can only draw in isolation is a topic you have memorised rather than learned.

Eight minutes on the map. Then five minutes comparing with a neighbour's, in this order:

- ☐ Name one node they have that you do not. Copy it.
- ☐ Name one node you have that they do not. Say it out loud — it is worth something.
- ☐ Name one edge you both drew but labeled differently. **Discuss it.** These are the expensive disagreements: two people think a detection control and a preventive control sit in different places. On the exam, one of you is wrong and it is worth knowing which.

## PART 3 — GAP LIST AND PAIR TEST (10 min)

**Gap list (4 min).** Transcribe your map gaps into a numbered list. Be specific: not "weak on access control" but "could not name the four models from memory in order."

**Pair test (6 min).** Swap gap lists with your neighbour. For their top two gaps, you write the answer **as if you were writing an exam response** — a specific control, a specific term, a specific artifact reading. Not a hint, not a pointer to a page. The actual content.

Then hand back. They read it and mark it: **would this earn the point on a rubric?** Mark honestly. A kind mark here is a wasted week.

**The instruction that makes this work:** if you cannot write the answer for your neighbour, you are not ready either. Writing it is the retrieval practice that closes the gap; reading someone else's is much weaker.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief.** Take weak topic 1 and apply it to one Taiwanese organization you have already studied — a semiconductor fab, a hospital system, an e-commerce platform, or a telecom operator.

**The question, in exam form:** *here is a real artifact from that organization's environment. Name the vulnerability, name the specific control, and name what would detect it.*

Because the topic is chosen from *your* data, the practical version of this question is: **is topic 1 something a Taiwanese organization would feel more acutely than most?** Semiconductor supply-chain risk, cross-border data-transfer obligations under PIPL, and physically accessible fab floor space are all places where our weak topic bites harder locally than it would in a generic scenario. One sentence, out loud, naming the local reason.
