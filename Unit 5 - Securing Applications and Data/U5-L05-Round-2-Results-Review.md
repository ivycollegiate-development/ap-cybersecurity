## PART 1 — PUT THE TWO ROUNDS SIDE BY SIDE (12 min)

Round 1: Apr 1, self-keyed over Spring Break. Round 2: Apr 16, taken in the room.

**Write the comparison in this exact form, one row per CED topic you have data for:**

| Topic | Round 1 | Round 2 | Change | What actually changed between Apr 1 and Apr 16? |
|-------|---------|---------|--------|---------------------------------------------|
| | | | | |

The last column is the one that matters and the one everybody skips. Fill it honestly:

- ☐ **I studied** between the rounds — a specific thing, not "reviewed."
- ☐ **Nothing changed in my preparation** — so any difference is noise.
- ☐ **I got *better* at the exam mechanics** (pacing, reading) without studying more.
- ☐ **I got *worse*** despite studying, which usually means the study was memorising rather than understanding.

**Say the honest caution out loud:** with one or two students, a difference of one or two items is **noise**, not signal. Do not build a strategy on a swing that small. Build it on the pattern across every item in that topic, and on the direction across several topics. This is a real analytical skill and it is one of the most transferable things in this course — knowing when your own data does not yet support a conclusion.

**But be precise about what the data *does* show.** A topic where you missed four items and still miss four items is a real signal regardless of sample size. A topic where you went from one miss to zero is a hint, not a conclusion.

## PART 2 — THE THREE WORST ITEMS, ITEM BY ITEM (18 min)

Not the topics. The **items**. Pull out the three you got most wrong across both rounds. For each, we work it as a group, and this is the method for the rest of the block:

1. **Read the item aloud, cold, without your key.** Someone reads it; nobody has the answer in front of them.
2. **Everyone commits to an answer in writing first.** Thirty seconds, alone, no talking. Committing matters — a discussion before you commit is just absorbing whoever spoke first.
3. **Then the key goes up, and the CED rationale is read for *every* option, including the wrong ones.** Do not just read why the right answer is right. The wrong options tell you where your model of the topic is wrong, and a wrong option that *seemed* right is a fact about you worth writing down.
4. **The required output, per item:** a line stating which *specific* misconception the item was testing and whether you had it.

**The write-up that earns the point is not "I didn't know the answer." It is a named belief.** For example: *I treated an IDS as a blocking control.* *I chose the more specific-sounding distractor over the correct general answer.* *I read "at rest" as "in transit."* Named beliefs can be corrected on Thursday. Unnamed ones cannot.

**Three items × six minutes. Keep it fast and it works.**

2. **If the targeted work did *not* move the numbers, the method was wrong, not the topic.** Most likely cause: you were re-reading and re-summarising instead of retrieving and writing. Retrieval is the part that moves scores. Say this out loud and change the method, not the target.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief.** One current TWNCERT or iThome item. **Question, and it is the exam question:** *for this incident, what did the organisation *fail to do* — name one missing control and one missing detection, and say which unit topic each belongs to.*

The reason this matters on the exam: a scenario question almost never asks you to identify what was done. It asks what *should* have been present and what *would have caught it*. A preventive control answer and a detective control answer are different points, and answering both when the item asks for one loses nothing but answering one when it asks for two does.

**Local angle, one sentence from a student:** pick a Taiwan-specific detail from the advisory — a local industry, a local consumer population, a local supply chain — and say what it changes about the answer. The best ones connect to semiconductor or critical-infrastructure exposure, or to the multilingual social-engineering angle that a US-centric scenario would not include.
