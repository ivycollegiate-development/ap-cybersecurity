## PART 1 — THE THREE THINGS YOU CARRY (12 min)

You have two behaviour cards from Thursday and Tuesday, and a worst-cell note from yesterday. Out of all of it, you get **three things** into the exam. Three. Not ten — ten does not survive contact with a timed exam, and trying to hold ten is itself a source of errors.

Write them, in order, on a single card:

1. **One behaviour card.** The imperative from your miss histogram — *name the layer before you name the control*, or *re-read the last sub-part before you stop writing*, or *every finding cites a field.* These are habits and they apply to any item.
2. **One specific term from your worst cell.** The one your sweeps showed you could not produce. Not a topic — a term, written exactly as the CED writes it.
3. **One specific control or vocabulary item from the unit that is most likely to be tested and that you have personally verified you know.** For most students this is a Unit 5 item — a vulnerability class, a signature property, an access-control discriminator.

**The card is the only thing you may bring into the exam room beyond what the exam allows, and it is a memory aid, not a crutch.** Three lines. If you find yourself wanting a fourth item, that is a sign the three are not specific enough — replace, do not add.

**We read them out.** Each student reads their three. It takes ninety seconds, it sounds trivial, and it works, because saying them aloud in this room on the last day is materially different from having them in a drawer on Wednesday morning.

## PART 2 — THE FOUR-FRAME CHECK (10 min)

Every scenario item this course has ever asked fits one of four frames. Knowing which frame you are in tells you what kind of answer is wanted, and it is worth more than any single fact.

**Frame 1 — Identify.** *What is this?* Name the term, the class, the model, the artifact type. **Answer shape:** a noun.

**Frame 2 — Explain.** *Why does this happen / what does this control do?* **Answer shape:** a causal sentence using the CED's vocabulary — "because…", "which means…". Two sentences at minimum; a one-sentence explanation is usually partial credit at best.

**Frame 3 — Recommend.** *What should they do?* **Answer shape:** a specific control plus the reason it addresses the specific issue in front of you. Generic controls score zero. Always pair the control with the vulnerability it fixes.

**Frame 4 — Justify / evaluate.** *Which is better, and why?* **Answer shape:** a comparison with a stated basis. Name the axis you are comparing on and then compare. "A is more secure than B" with no basis is zero; "B, because it constrains access by role rather than by owner, which survives staff turnover" is a full answer.

**The drill (8 min):** four items, one per frame, two minutes each, written cold. For each, before answering, **write the frame label at the top.** Labelling the frame is the scored skill — it is what stops you writing an explanation when the item asked for a recommendation.

## PART 3 — THE 60-SECOND MENTAL REHEARSAL (10 min)

**This is the last content activity of the year.** We do not review material. We rehearse the *procedure*, because procedure is what fails under pressure.

**Scenario, out loud, in order. Answer each in sixty seconds, on the card's back:**

- You are handed a log excerpt with a suspicious request in it. **What is your first move?** *(Read it straight through. Name the artifact. Establish the baseline. Cite the field.)*
- You are handed a config with a setting you dislike. **What do you write?** *(The setting, the risk it creates, the safer value — and a separate line for what you cannot verify from the file alone.)*
- You are given a scenario and asked for one control. **What do you write?** *(Name the layer. Name the control. Name the vulnerability it addresses. Three clauses.)*
- You are 20 minutes into a multi-part item with parts left. **What do you do?** *(Move on immediately. Come back if time remains. A blank is a guaranteed zero; a rough answer is frequently a point.)*
- You read an item and genuinely do not know. **What do you do?** *(Eliminate. Two options you can rule out beats a blank. And: never abandon the rest of the item because of one part.)*

**The last two are the ones that decide scores**, and both are pure decisions, not knowledge. Nothing new has to be learned to make them.

## PART 4 — LOGISTICS (10 min)

**Time and place.** AP Cybersecurity exam, **Wednesday May 5, 2027, 8:00 AM.** Arrive by **7:40** — early, not on time, because a room you are not yet in is a problem you do need. If you are running late or something has gone wrong, tell me or tell the front office **the moment you know**, not at 7:55.

**Bring:**
- ☐ Photo ID, as required for the exam room
- ☐ Two sharpened pencils and an eraser — a broken pencil mid-item is a real and stupid way to lose points
- ☐ A clear water bottle if the room permits it
- ☐ Your three-line card, if the testing platform permits a personal reference card — **check the current College Board rules yourself and tell me which way it goes.** If it is not permitted, the card stays home and you rely on rehearsal.

**Do not bring:** anything you would be upset to explain, any notes beyond what is explicitly permitted, and no expectation that the room will have working wi-fi for anything.

**On the platform:** the exam runs digitally. Before Wednesday, **complete at least one full practice run in the actual platform** if you have not already, so that the interface is not new to you on exam day. Know how to select text, navigate between parts, flag a question for return, and expand the reading pane. **The single most common avoidable loss in a digital exam is a navigation mistake, not a knowledge gap.** Spend the twenty minutes tonight in the platform.

**Timing.** The exam has two portions, multiple choice then free response. The free-response portion is where the points are concentrated, and the multiple-choice portion runs faster than most students pace for. Plan to finish the multiple-choice portion with time left rather than using every minute — the pressure of a clock running down on the section that follows is a real, avoidable loss.

**On the day itself:**
- ☐ Sleep. The single highest-yield exam-preparation action available to you, and the one nobody does.
- ☐ Eat something. A hungry brain misreads.
- ☐ Arrive by 7:40.
- ☐ Read the item, then answer. Every time, including the easy ones.
- ☐ Flag and move if stuck. Never lose a whole item to ninety wasted seconds.

**Housekeeping for the rest of the year — this matters, so listen.** The exam is over on Wednesday, and then this course continues. **The post-exam block is May 6 through May 28 — thirteen school days, and the last block of the year.** From Thursday May 6 the work changes completely: no new CED content, no exam review, no more graded assessments in the exam categories. What replaces it is portfolio and career work — real deliverables you can show an employer or put on a college application, built on the writeups you have already produced in the Saturday CTF sessions. It is assessed as portfolio work and participation, and Thursday May 6 is the exam debrief. **You are not finished on Wednesday. Wednesday is the middle.**

**And one more date, so nobody is surprised:** the final portfolio presentations are **Friday May 28.** Plan for it now.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

**3-minute brief, the last of the year.** We have opened every lesson this year with Taiwan — the fab, the critical infrastructure, the phishing waves, the incident-response obligations, the semiconductor supply chain, the data-protection law. It is worth saying why, one last time, before the exam.

**The question to sit with on Wednesday when you are asked a scenario:** *this item will describe a fictional organisation in a fictional place. The reasoning it wants is the same reasoning a real incident response in Taipei or Hsinchu or Kaohsiung demands.* Nothing in the rubric changes because the scenario is set in Taiwan — and that is exactly the point. **The local knowledge is what makes you better at the generic exam, not instead of it.** You know what a fab floor, a local phishing lure, and a PIPL notification obligation actually look like. That is not decoration in your interview; it is the reason you are more employable than the candidate who memorised the same CED text in a vacuum.

One sentence each, out loud, before you pack up: name one thing this year you could not have learned from a textbook, and one thing the textbook taught you that turned out to matter more than you expected.
